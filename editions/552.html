AI Daily Briefing — July 26, 2026

The AI displacement wave continues to crest as Monday.com joins a growing roster of companies citing AI in layoff announcements, while researchers and institutions grapple with what agentic AI systems are actually capable of — and what safeguards are missing. Today's digest spans workforce disruption, campus surveillance, legal education reform, and a sobering audit of agentic coding frameworks.

Industry Moves

Monday.com is the latest tech company to blame AI for layoffs — and TechCrunch is keeping a running list of more than 20 others that have done the same in 2026. The pattern is becoming impossible to ignore: AI-as-stated-cause is now a standard line in layoff communications, raising questions about how much is genuine automation and how much is convenient framing. For workers and policymakers alike, the aggregate picture is sobering regardless of the breakdown.

Agentic AI & Benchmarks

Dan Luu published a characteristically rigorous set of notes on agentic coding, covering agentic test processes, LLM benchmarks, and the practical realities of where these systems succeed and fail. The piece is a useful corrective to hype-heavy coverage, digging into how benchmark design shapes perceived capability and where agentic coding pipelines still break down in real workflows. Worth reading slowly if you're building with or evaluating coding agents.

Meanwhile, a developer running a security-minded audit of their own AI agent framework found a striking number of destructive and high-consequence actions lurking in the codebase — the kind that can cause real damage if an agent misfires or is manipulated. The post is a wake-up call for anyone shipping agentic systems without explicit action-risk classification, and underscores that "agentic" and "safe by default" are still far from synonymous.

AI & Society

Bruce Schneier flags that MIT is set to become a testbed for AI-powered video surveillance, a development that carries extra weight given the institution's outsized influence on both AI research and campus policy norms. Schneier's framing is pointed: when a premier research university normalizes pervasive AI surveillance on its own grounds, it sets a precedent that ripples outward. The privacy implications extend well beyond any single campus.

The University of Chicago Law School has released an AI strategy statement rethinking legal education for the AI era — a sign that elite institutions are moving past hand-wringing toward concrete curricular and pedagogical commitments. How law schools train the next generation of attorneys will have downstream consequences for how AI regulation, liability, and rights are shaped in courtrooms and legislatures over the coming decade.

Worth Watching

The disappearance of Agatha Christie — amnesia, suicide attempt, or calculated retreat? — surfaced on Hacker News this week, likely because AI tools are increasingly being applied to cold historical mysteries. Not strictly an AI story, but the intersection of LLM-powered historical analysis and unsolved cases is a space worth tracking as these tools mature.
